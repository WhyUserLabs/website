Synthetic personas vs. real buyer panels

The argument is usually framed as real versus fake. That framing is wrong and it costs teams money. The useful question is which kind of error you can afford this week.

The framing that wastes money

“Real buyers or synthetic ones” sounds like a question about truth. It is actually a question about which error you can afford, and the two methods fail in opposite directions.

  • A panel’s failure mode is stated preference. People are reliable reporters of what confuses them and unreliable predictors of what they will do. They say they prefer headline A and click headline B.
  • A simulation’s failure mode is confident emptiness. Compiled from thin evidence, it produces a fluent, well-structured answer about nothing, and there is no visible signal that this has happened.

Both errors are survivable. Neither is survivable if you do not know which one you are exposed to.

Four questions to ask any synthetic persona vendor

Most of the difference between a useful synthetic persona and an expensive random-answer generator sits in these four answers. Ask them of us too.

  1. What is it compiled from, specifically? “Trained on B2B data” is not an answer. Reviews, call transcripts, community threads, competitor comparisons — name the sources, and show the source on each finding.
  2. Is it deterministic? Ask them to run the same page twice. If the verdict changes, you cannot use it to test an edit, because you can never tell your change from the model’s mood.
  3. What is the published hit rate, and where are the misses? Any vendor can quote an accuracy number. Ask whether the predictions were sealed before the outcome existed, who graded them, and whether misses stayed in the denominator. See evidence ledger.
  4. What does it refuse to claim? A vendor who will not name a limitation has not found one yet, which tells you how much they have tested.

Three questions to ask a panel vendor

  1. How are respondents verified? B2B targeting is only as good as the vetting behind it. “Works in IT” is not a buying committee role.
  2. Can I reach the roles that actually block deals? Security reviewers and economic buyers are the hardest profiles to recruit and the ones most likely to kill your deal.
  3. What happens to my sample size when I segment by role? A 50-person panel across five roles is ten people per role, and ten is not many when the roles disagree.

What a simulation genuinely cannot do

Worth being blunt, since we sell one.

  • It cannot replace talking to customers. It is compiled from customer evidence, so it sits downstream of it. Thin evidence in, confident nothing out.
  • It cannot give you absolute numbers. Rank and direction are defensible. A precise conversion-rate prediction is not, and one wrong magnitude destroys the credibility of every correct finding next to it.
  • It inherits the bias of its corpus. Public reviews skew toward the delighted and the furious; the quiet middle is under-represented. A good system says so instead of smoothing it over.
  • It cannot surprise you from outside its evidence. A real person occasionally says the thing nobody modelled. That is worth paying for.

The sequence

If you can run both, the order is not arbitrary. Run the cheap, repeatable, committee-aware method first, on many versions. Run the expensive, human, surprising method second, on the survivor. Then let live traffic settle it.

  1. Simulate — kill the structural failures. Missing proof for a role, no baseline number, a broken scent trail between ad and page. Iterate freely; it costs almost nothing.
  2. Panel — spend real money on the question humans answer best: does this land, in these words, with this person.
  3. A/B — the only method that measures what actually happened.

Teams who invert steps one and two spend panel budget discovering that the page has no security signal. That is a finding you should never pay a human to give you.

Common questions

Are synthetic personas better than real buyer panels?

Neither is better. They fail in opposite directions: a panel measures stated preference, which diverges from behaviour, while a simulation compiled from thin evidence produces a confident answer about nothing. The useful question is which error you can afford this week.

What should I ask a synthetic persona vendor before buying?

What the personas are compiled from, specifically; whether the system is deterministic so the same input returns the same verdict; what the published hit rate is, whether predictions were sealed before the outcome existed, and whether misses stayed in the denominator; and what the vendor refuses to claim.

Can synthetic personas replace customer interviews?

No. They are compiled from customer evidence, so they sit downstream of it. If the underlying evidence is thin, the simulation is fluent and empty, and nothing in the output signals that.

In what order should I run simulation, panel, and A/B testing?

Simulate first to kill structural failures cheaply across many versions, panel second on the survivor for the resonance question humans answer best, and A/B last because it is the only method that measures what actually happened.
