How to evaluate a pre-launch testing method

Four things a measurement has to do. Twelve questions that test them. Ask any tool, including us. Our answers are below, and we lose three rows.

The four

Every method on your list claims to measure something. A measurement has to do four things. Otherwise it is an opinion with a number on it.

The four properties

Repeats. Same page in, same answer out.

Shows its source. Every quote traced to a real page.

Reads like your buyer. Scans, hunts, abandons. Never patient.

Remembers. Week 4 knows what week 1 did not.

That order matters. Fail the first one and you cannot check the other three, because you have nothing stable to check against.

1. Does it repeat?

This is the question that decides everything. It is also the cheapest to answer.

The four-minute test. Run it on every tool, including ours.

Open two fresh sessions with no shared history. Paste the same page into both. Ask the same question. Compare.

If they disagree about what is broken, you have an opinion generator. That is a fine thing to own. It is not a thing you can spend budget against.

Ask:

  • Q1. Run the same asset twice in two fresh sessions. Do the verdicts agree?
  • Q2. Fix one section and re-run. Does it tell you whether that fix worked? Or does it review the whole page again and hand you a new list?
  • Q3. When the vendor upgrades their model, does last month's verdict change?

Q2 is the one people skip. It matters most. If the tool returns a fresh read every time, you cannot separate your change from its variation. You are guessing again, which is what you were trying to stop doing.

Q3 catches something quieter. If a verdict is not frozen under the model that produced it, a deploy on the vendor's side can rewrite a decision you already made and defended.

Our answer

Yes to all three. A re-run replays sealed findings instead of recomputing them. Untouched parts of the page come back identical. Only what you edited can move. If our environment drifts, we say so on the report and hide the comparison rather than quietly serving you a different answer. Determinism and replay →

2. Does it show its source?

A made-up quote and a real quote look the same on screen. The only difference is whether anyone checked.

Ask:

  • Q4. Can it trace any quote to a string that exists in a named document?
  • Q5. Does it tell you who wrote the evidence? A buyer, or a rival's comparison page?
  • Q6. Does it flag a finding that rests on one source?

Q5 is the one nobody asks. Point any research agent at a B2B category and much of what it finds was written by companies that sell against you. Comparison pages are built to be found. That is their job.

A tool with no sense of authorship reads a competitor's landing page and a buyer review the same way. To it, both are text. So it reasons about your rival's positioning as if a customer said it.

You will not see an error. You will see a confident finding, well written, with a link.

Our answer

Yes to all three. We learned Q5 the hard way. When we first built our collection pipeline, about a quarter of the findings in our own corpus had been written by competing vendors, and the system had no way to say so. One brief was entirely competitor-authored and still carried a "community voice" label. So we built author classification. Every source now carries who wrote it, all the way into the simulation. We keep competitor pages, because your buyers read them too. We never promote them to fact. Quotes are checked against the fetched document, not taken on trust. 3. Does it read like your buyer?

A general AI assistant is the most patient reader alive, and none of your buyers are.

It starts at the top. It reads to the bottom. It does not get bored at the fold. It does not open three competitor tabs halfway down. It does not leave because the pricing link went to a form.

It is the best possible reader of your page. That is the problem.

Ask:

  • Q7. What reader does it assume? One patient reader, or the mix your channel actually sends?
  • Q8. Does it model scanning, hunting and abandoning? Or only careful reading?
  • Q9. Does one blocking role sink the verdict, or is everything averaged?
Cold LinkedIn traffic, by state

82% distracted. Headlines only. Will not scroll.

15% skeptical. Burned before. Wants proof, not adjectives.

3% actively shopping. Reads properly. Forgives a weak hook.

Those come from published benchmarks, not a hunch. They move by channel. Cold Google Search almost inverts, because someone who typed your category into a search box has already told you they are in market.

A tool that assumes one patient reader is measuring a group that barely exists in your paid traffic.

Q9 matters because averaging hides the expensive failure. Committees do not average. One role with a veto sinks a deal four other roles liked.

Our answer

Every role runs across all three states, weighted to the channel you are buying. Each agent scans, hunts or abandons instead of reading dutifully to the end. Roles combine by veto rules in code, not by blending scores. Behavioural state →

4. Does it remember?

This is what separates a tool you use once from something you can run a programme on.

Ask:

  • Q10. Does week 4 know anything week 1 did not?
  • Q11. Is there a published hit rate? Are the misses still in the denominator?
  • Q12. Are claims sealed before the outcome exists?

There is a version of Q10 that catches almost everything. Ask the vendor this:

The question to ask

"If a pricing objection shows up in your buyer evidence this week, can you tell me it is the same objection that showed up three weeks ago?"

Most methods cannot. The reason is structural, not careless. A language model produces text with no identity. The same idea comes back worded differently every time, from a different source, with no way to know it is the same idea.

We tested this on four weeks of real buyer evidence for one customer. Zero findings carried over between weeks. Not few. Zero. Yet the themes were there in nearly every week, wearing different words from different sites.

So a tool pointed at fresh evidence every Monday reports a different market every Monday. It is not wrong. It has no way to know it is looking at the same thing.

Q12 is the difference between a track record and a claim. Grading a prediction after you know the result is not grading.

Our answer

Findings are matched by meaning, not wording, so a signal keeps its identity across weeks even when every quote behind it is new. Buyers are not files. They are feeds. Each one re-compiles from live evidence on every run, so when your market moves they move. Claims are sealed before the campaign runs, then graded HIT or MISS by your team against your own data. Never by us. Misses stay in the denominator. 473 claims graded, 84% accurate →

Where we lose

A guide whose author wins every row is a brochure. Here are three we do not win.

  • Speed. A general AI assistant answers in seconds. We take about thirty minutes. For a gut check before lunch, use the assistant.
  • Writing. We do not write copy and are not trying to. Drafting, cutting, twenty headline variants: that is what an assistant is genuinely great at.
  • Magnitude. We tell you whether a call was right. We do not yet tell you by how much. A live A/B test beats us on that.

How to run this

You do not need a bake-off. It is an afternoon.

  • Q1 costs four minutes. Two sessions, same page, compare. Do it before any vendor call.
  • Q4 and Q5 cost one email. Ask each vendor to trace one quote and name who wrote it. The answers will not resemble each other.
  • Q11 costs one search. Either the number is published with its misses, or it is not.
  • For us: pick three campaigns you already ran and know the results of. Do not tell us which won. We predict the ranking. You check it.

Common questions

Can I use a general AI assistant to test a landing page before launch?

For drafting and variants, yes. For a spend decision, it fails Q1. Ask it the same page twice in two fresh sessions and you get two different answers. You cannot tell whether your change worked or the model just sampled differently. It is also the most patient reader alive, which is the opposite of your traffic.

What if we connect the assistant to our own data?

You can, and it helps. Attaching evidence is the easy part now. What follows is not.

A model reads your evidence well. It cannot tell you it read the same thing last week. It cannot tell a buyer's words from a rival's marketing. And it cannot reach the evidence that decides most deals, which sits in competitor reviews and community threads. Your CRM and call recordings look inward. Everyone in them already replied to you.

Why does repeating matter so much?

Because attribution depends on it. If the answer moves on an unchanged page, it cannot tell you anything about a changed one. Every other question here assumes you have a stable reading to compare against.

Is a high hit rate enough?

No. Ask three things. Are the misses still counted? Were claims sealed before the outcome existed? Who did the grading? A rate graded by the vendor, after the fact, with failures removed, is marketing.
